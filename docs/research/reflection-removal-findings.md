# Redlamp: open models for Remove Reflections (RM-22)

A survey of open single-image reflection removal models Redlamp could use for Remove Reflections, which removes reflections from photos taken through glass as Lightroom's Reflection Removal does; a check of WindowSeat, which leads the published methods, on this Mac; and a prototype that keeps the photo's full resolution by subtracting the reflection layer the model finds rather than upsampling its output. Tracker: RM-22, [#521](https://github.com/pdcgomes/redlamp/issues/521); the glass benchmark set is part of INF-14, [#522](https://github.com/pdcgomes/redlamp/issues/522).

Prepared 1 November 2026. Every licence statement below was read on that date from a primary source: the repository's licence through GitHub's licence API and the file itself, the Hugging Face model card and its metadata (`https://huggingface.co/api/models/<id>`), the dataset's terms page, or the data section of the paper. The verdicts use the [open-model findings](open-model-findings.md)' legend (Shippable, Problematic, Not allowed) under the rules since accepted: follow the weights' licence and never ship a model trained directly on Places2 (DEC-24); a model whose training data is undisclosed may be an opt-in download, labelled (DEC-23). This is an engineering survey, not legal advice. Measurements were made on the M1 Ultra (128 GB) while two other agents used it, so each timing gives the load average it was taken at. The prototypes are in [research/prototypes/reflections](../../research/prototypes/reflections/README.md).

## 1. Summary

- **WindowSeat is the one shippable model, and it leads on quality.** Its code and its LoRA are Apache-2.0, and so is the model it adapts, Qwen-Image-Edit-2509. It was trained on the Nature and Real pairs every method uses and on 25,000 renders of glass made from Poly Haven's CC0 HDRIs and COCO images; the base model's own data is undisclosed, so DEC-23 applies: an opt-in download, labelled. On SIR², which it didn't train on, it leads the published methods by about 2 dB (28.84 against 26.90). The paper's main model is built on FLUX.1 Kontext and isn't released; the Apache-2.0 one on Qwen scores within 0.15 dB of it.
- **Nothing smaller is both open and close.** GFRRN (MIT, a Swin-L network) is shippable under DEC-24 but 1.5 dB behind on SIR²; DAI and FUMO relabel Stable Diffusion 2 weights as Apache-2.0; PRISM, a FLUX.2 [klein] 4B fine-tune within 0.9 dB of WindowSeat that would run on Redlamp's existing port, says in its README that its code and weights are non-commercial while its LICENSE file says Apache-2.0; RDNet, DSIT and the NTIRE 2026 winners built on them have no licence at all; everything on FLUX.1 Kontext is non-commercial.
- **WindowSeat runs on this Mac through MLX.** It needs only the base model's transformer and VAE, the LoRA and a 2.4 MB prompt embedding shipped with it: no text or vision-language encoder. With the LoRA fused and the transformer at 4 bits, the download is 13.5 GB and a 768-pixel tile takes 7.5 s on the M1 Ultra at a load average of 59, so about 15 s for a 3:2 photo. Memory peaks at 22 GB as mflux runs it, about 14 GB of it the transformer's; the rest is a VAE a port would convolve in bands, as RedlampGenerative's FLUX VAE does. That makes it a Mac-only feature for 32 GB or more. The MLX port matches diffusers' to 48.9 dB in bfloat16; 4 bits cost 0.7 dB on the composites against 8 bits.
- **The full-resolution subtraction is the way to use it.** On twelve composites with an exact truth, the model's output upsampled to the photo's size scores below the photo itself (19.66 dB and SSIM 0.635, against 21.20 and 0.913), because it is soft and brighter than the scene. Estimating the reflection layer at the model's resolution, correcting the model's brightening, and subtracting the layer from the full-size photo in linear light scores 27.97 dB and 0.961, better than the photo on every case and with more fine detail than it, and gives an Amount that needs no new run of the model. Where a reflection is itself sharp, its finest detail stays.

**NTIRE 2026's challenge on reflection removal in the wild** ([report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.10321)) ranked eleven teams on the OpenRR-5k test set by human scores. The first three all build on RDNet: RRay cascades RDNet into NAFNet (PSNR 36.17 on the validation set, score 4.45); Xreflect Master scales RDNet up and distils WindowSeat's and DAI's outputs into it (36.05, 4.31); AIIALab refines RDNet in stages (35.38, 4.23). None of their code is usable: RDNet has no licence, AIIALab's repository is empty, the fourth-placed CompSIRS says "Code will be available soon", and OpenRR-5k itself is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0 ([HF](https://huggingface.co/datasets/qiuzhangTiTi/OpenRR-5k)). The report notes that the leading methods trained on data beyond OpenRR-5k, and that PSNR follows the human scores only loosely.

**Classical methods** (relative smoothness, Li and Brown 2014; convex reflection suppression, Yang et al. 2019) have no weights to license and can be implemented from their papers, but they assume a reflection blurrier than the scene and fail where it isn't. They are the only fallback that needs no download.

**For a model of Redlamp's own:** WindowSeat's code includes its Blender rendering pipeline ([dataset_generation](https://github.com/huawei-bayerlab/windowseat-reflection-removal/tree/main/dataset_generation), Apache-2.0). Fed with Poly Haven's CC0 HDRIs and the CC0 photo sets the open-model findings list (Megalith-10M, PD12M), it would make training pairs with no research-only images, and its recipe trained the LoRA in a day on one GPU (paper §4.1). PRISM shows FLUX.2 [klein] 4B, which Redlamp already downloads for Generative Remove, can be fine-tuned to within 0.9 dB of WindowSeat on SIR².

## 3. WindowSeat on the Mac

### 3.1 How it runs

WindowSeat's inference ([windowseat_inference.py](https://github.com/huawei-bayerlab/windowseat-reflection-removal/blob/main/windowseat_inference.py)) needs only the base model's transformer and VAE, the LoRA and the shipped prompt embedding: the photo's VAE latent is packed into 2 × 2 patches as the transformer's only image stream (no reference or noise stream), the timestep is 0.499 with no guidance, and the output latent is the input less the predicted velocity. Neither the Qwen2.5-VL text encoder nor its vision part runs, so the 16.6 GB encoder needn't be downloaded. The authors run the transformer in 4-bit NF4 through bitsandbytes on a 24 GB CUDA GPU; that path doesn't exist on the Mac.

`windowseat_mlx.py` runs the same pass through mflux 0.24.0 (MIT) on MLX 0.32.4. mflux's Qwen-Image LoRA support covers each block's attention and MLP layers but not `img_in`, `txt_in` and `norm_out.linear`, which WindowSeat's LoRA also adapts (1,446 tensors in all; `proj_out` is in its config but not in its weights), so the script adds each of the 723 B·A products to its base weight in float32 and quantises afterwards, every linear layer but those three and `proj_out`, with the modulation layers kept at 8 bits under a 4-bit model as mflux does. Fusing and quantising takes 50 seconds. The port is checked against diffusers' own pipeline on PyTorch's Metal backend in bfloat16 (`windowseat_torch.py`) on six of WindowSeat's example photos at 768 pixels:

| MLX variant | Against diffusers in bfloat16 | Transformer file |
|---|---|---|
| bfloat16 | 48.9 dB (44.8 to 55.4) | 40.9 GB |
| 8-bit | 46.4 dB (42.2 to 53.6) | 21.7 GB |
| 4-bit (modulation at 8) | 32.6 dB (27.6 to 40.2) | 13.2 GB |

Unlike FLUX.2 [klein] 4B's fill, where 4 bits stayed 41 to 46 dB from float32, 4 bits change WindowSeat's output visibly in places; §4 measures what that costs against the truth.

### 3.2 Time and memory

One square tile, the 4-bit model, evaluated block by block (`windowseat_mlx.py bench`, two runs each, the second shown; loaded from the SSD in 4 to 5 seconds). MLX's peak memory includes the 13.2 GB of weights. Measured at a load average of 58 to 60, with the RM-20 agent's FLUX.2 fills sharing the GPU, so an idle M1 Ultra would be somewhat faster:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 512 × 512 | 0.25 s | 2.82 s | 0.94 s | 4.0 s | 21.4 GB (13.8 GB) |
| 768 × 768 | 0.56 s | 5.45 s | 1.50 s | 7.5 s | 22.3 GB (14.1 GB) |
| 1024 × 1024 | 1.05 s | 9.66 s | 3.99 s | 14.7 s | 29.1 GB (14.5 GB) |

- As one graph rather than block by block, a 768-pixel tile took 7.1 s at a load of 54: evaluating each block costs about 4%.
- 8 bits: 7.9 s a 768-pixel tile (transformer 5.4 s) at a load of 51, as fast as 4 bits, with a peak of 30.8 GB (22.6 GB in the transformer stage). bfloat16 in MLX: 16 to 33 s (load 43 to 59). diffusers on the Metal backend in bfloat16: 14 s a tile, 49 GB of GPU memory, and 48 s to load (load 66 to 69).
- A 3:2 photo at 768 pixels is two tiles, so about 15 s on this Mac with the model loaded, plus the full-resolution step, which in NumPy takes 10 to 25 s for 12 MP and would take a fraction of a second on the GPU. With the GPU shared, the glass runs took 8 to 16 s a tile at 512 and 768, and 47 to 59 s at 1,024.
- Assessment: an M1 Pro or M2 Pro has a quarter to a third of the M1 Ultra's GPU, so a photo would take about a minute there; an M3 or M4 Max, about the same as here.

### 3.3 Which Macs, and the download

**The download** at 4 bits is 13.5 GB: the transformer with the LoRA fused (13.24 GB), the VAE (0.25 GB) and the prompt embedding (2.4 MB), the transformer in seven parts as release assets under GitHub's 2 GB a file (DEC-26). At 8 bits it is 22.0 GB. For comparison, Generative Remove's FLUX.2 [klein] 4B is 2.41 GB. Keeping the modulation layers at 4 bits as well would save about 1.8 GB; its quality wasn't measured.

**Memory.** The weights take 13.2 GB and the transformer's pass about 1.3 GB more, at every size measured (14.5 GB peak). What makes the peak larger as mflux runs it is the VAE: its 3D convolutions over a single frame are unfolded by MLX into a copy per tap of the kernel, as FLUX.2's VAE was before RedlampGenerative convolved it in bands (the [generative fill design](../plans/2026-10-05-generative-fill-design.md#what-a-fill-costs)). A port that convolves in bands, and drops the two of the three temporal taps that only ever see the causal padding of a single frame, should keep the peak near the transformer's.

Assessment: as mflux runs it, the 4-bit model needs a Mac with at least 32 GB at 768 pixels (22 GB peak, with the app and macOS beside it) and 48 GB at 1024 (29 GB). Ported with a banded VAE, about 16 GB at any size, which makes 32 GB comfortable and 24 GB possible but tight; 16 GB Macs can't hold it, since the weights alone are 13.2 GB. Among Apple silicon Macs, 32 GB or more means the Pro, Max and Ultra chips' larger configurations and an M4 or later with 32 GB; base M1 to M3 Macs top out at 16 or 24 GB. The 8-bit model needs 48 GB.

### 3.4 What went wrong on a shared GPU

Twice, a tile came back as NaN, and once MLX stopped with "Command buffer execution failed: Impacting Interactivity": macOS cancels a GPU command buffer that runs too long while the display needs the GPU, and the whole 60-block pass was one lazily built graph. Evaluating after each block keeps every command buffer short, and the prototype now does that and runs a failed tile again. A Redlamp port has to do the same, as RedlampGenerative's FLUX port does step by step, and check its output for NaN before it reaches an edit.

## 4. Keeping full resolution

### 4.1 The method

The model works on the photo scaled so its short side is 768 pixels (WindowSeat's training size), in square tiles of that side: two for a 3:2 photo. Its own script then upsamples the result to the photo's size with Lanczos, which throws away everything finer than the working size, from a 12 MP photo four times over on each side and from a 50 MP one eight. `fullres.py` uses the model's output only for what it is good at, finding the reflection, and keeps the photo's own pixels:

1. The photo is averaged down in linear light to the working size, I_w, and goes to the model sRGB-encoded; its output O_w comes back and is linearised.
2. **Drift.** WindowSeat also brightens the transmitted scene: on every composite its output is 13 to 50% brighter than the truth (the median of output over truth, per channel), and on the two real photos checked (the cake case and the laundrette) its mean is 22% and 17% above its input's in linear light, though it removed light. Assessment: its training renders take the glass away entirely, so it learned to undo the light the glass itself absorbs, which Lightroom and the photographer keep. Each channel of O_w is therefore scaled by a gain, the 0.2 quantile of I_w / O_w over the well-exposed pixels with both smoothed: a reflection only ever adds light, so the low end of that ratio is where the photo has the least of it.
3. **The reflection layer** at the working size is R_w = max(I_w − O', 0), smoothed by a Gaussian of 2 working pixels: what differs at the finest working scale is mostly the model's own resynthesised texture, not reflection.
4. **Full resolution.** R_w is upsampled (bicubic, in linear light) and subtracted from the full-size photo: T = I − amount · R. Amount 1 is the result; 0 is the photo; a negative amount gives (1 − |a|) · I + |a| · R, the reflection alone at −1, as Lightroom's −100 does. Changing the Amount needs no new run of the model, and what an edit would store is R_w: about a megapixel, smooth, like a generated fill's pixels (RM-10).

### 4.2 Results on the composites

Against the truth at full resolution in sRGB, mean over the twelve composites (`research/benchmarks/results/glass-2026-11-01.json`, which has every case). Detail PSNR is the PSNR of what the image has finer than a Gaussian of 2 pixels, which shows what upsampling loses.

| Method | PSNR | SSIM | Detail PSNR |
|---|---|---|---|
| The photo through glass, untouched | 21.20 | 0.913 | 41.91 |
| Model output upsampled, 4 bits, 768 (WindowSeat's own way) | 19.66 | 0.635 | 28.22 |
| The same with the drift gain applied first | 22.41 | 0.640 | 28.25 |
| **Subtraction, 4 bits, 768** | **27.97** | **0.961** | **43.41** |
| Subtraction, 8 bits, 768 | 28.69 | 0.963 | 43.66 |
| Subtraction, 4 bits, 512 | 27.04 | 0.953 | 43.01 |
| Subtraction, 4 bits, 1,024 | 28.39 | 0.966 | 43.68 |
| Model output upsampled, 4 bits, 1,024 | 20.23 | 0.678 | 28.63 |
| Subtraction without the drift gain | 26.69 | 0.962 | 44.17 |
| Subtraction with an affine drift fit instead | 26.19 | 0.937 | 42.67 |
| Subtraction without smoothing R_w | 26.38 | 0.917 | 37.29 |
| Subtraction leaving clipped pixels out of R | 27.53 | 0.958 | 41.80 |
| Subtraction with the model's output in clipped pixels | 25.63 | 0.889 | 36.30 |

The subtraction beats the untouched photo on every composite, by 0.8 dB (the lamp over a wall of pictures) to 11.2 dB (the double image), and the upsampled output on every one, by 5.6 dB on average (1.9 to 10.1) when that has the same drift correction. The upsampled output loses the photo's detail (28.2 dB of detail PSNR against the photo's 41.9) and gains only what the reflection's removal brings at large scales; the subtraction keeps all of the first and nearly all of the second. The JPEG compression of the transmitted photos is part of their truth, so only the subtraction can keep it, which flatters it slightly against the upsampled output in PSNR, though not in what can be seen.

What the ablations show, all on the 4-bit model at 768 pixels:

- **The drift gain** is worth 1.3 dB (26.69 without it). An affine fit on the lower envelope of I_w against O_w scored worse than none (26.19). With the true gain, read from the truth, the subtraction reaches 29.0 dB on eleven of the composites (all but the 50 MP one) against 28.1 with the estimated one, so a better estimate is worth about another dB; a tone curve fitted on the lower envelope and an iterative gain did no better than the simple one. These comparisons, and the detail gate in §4.3, were one-off scripts over the cached 4-bit outputs and aren't kept. The estimate overcorrects photos whose reflection is faint or small (the faint reflection and the two lamps), where the low end of the ratio falls below the true gain.
- **Smoothing R_w** by 2 working pixels is worth 1.6 dB and 0.044 of SSIM (26.38 and 0.917 without it). Without it, the model's texture comes back as speckle.
- **Clipped highlights** need nothing special. Leaving their pixels out of R's upsampling cost 0.4 dB, and putting the corrected model output in them cost 2.3 dB and 0.07 of SSIM: where the photo is white, the subtraction already gives the model's estimate in flat areas, and the replacement only spread the model's softness into the texture around them. That holds for these composites, whose clipped areas are a few percent of the frame; a photo whose sky behind the glass is blown out isn't among them.
- **The tile size.** At 512 pixels the subtraction loses 0.9 dB against 768; at 1,024, though the model was trained at 768, it gains 0.4 dB and 0.005 of SSIM, for about twice the time a tile and 29 GB of peak memory as mflux runs it. 8 bits at 768 gain 0.7 dB over 4 bits.

### 4.3 The real photos and the blind picks

On the ten real photos the drift gain came out below 1 in every channel, between 0.64 and 0.98: over most of each photo the model's output is brighter than the photo, and the subtraction keeps the photo's exposure where the upsampled output doesn't. The blind picks put the upsampled output beside the subtraction, both from the 4-bit model at 768 pixels, in two 100% crops a photo: where the estimated reflection peaks, and where the photo's detail and the reflection overlap; one photo gave only one distinct crop, so there are 19 pairs. The canvas is `picks-glass-2026-11-01.canvas.tsx` in Cursor's canvases folder for this repository; `picks.py analyse glass-2026-11-01` reads the marks.

Assessment, from looking at a few crops myself: the subtraction keeps the photo's sharpness and grain and the upsampled output is plainly soft, but where the reflection is itself sharp, a building reflected in a shop window in focus, the subtraction leaves the reflection's fine detail (window frames, edges) over the scene, because the reflection layer is only known at the working size. The upsampled output removes those edges with everything else. Attenuating the photo's finest detail where the model's output has less mid-scale detail than the photo did nothing useful (0.2 dB lower on the composites, and the window frames stayed). Working nearer the photo's own resolution does more: with the working size twice 768 on the short side, in nine to twelve tiles of 768 as WindowSeat's `--more-tiles` does, the window frames on the Law Hub crop are fainter, at about four and a half times the time (4.4 minutes for that photo with the GPU shared). On the four composites tried that way (the ghost, the sharp towers, the double image and the bright sky) it made no measurable difference to the subtraction (26.55 dB against 26.51; the upsampled output gained 1.8 dB), since their reflections are mostly soft. Like Lightroom's Best quality, it is a choice between time and the last of the reflection's detail, and the picks don't include it yet.

### 4.4 Limits

- Sharp reflections keep their finest detail, as above.
- The drift gain is an estimate, and overcorrects faint or small reflections by darkening the whole photo slightly.
- The composites' reflections are made, not photographed; real glass also refracts, ghosts at angles and reflects more at grazing angles. The real photos' picks and Lightroom's results are the check on that.
- Clipped areas larger than a few percent of the frame haven't been tested.

## 5. The glass benchmark set (INF-14)

`research/benchmarks/glass.json` lists 22 cases in the README's format, checked with `benchmarks.py list --set glass`:

- **Twelve composites with an exact truth**, made by `research/prototypes/reflections/make_glass.py` from twelve CC0 photos on Wikimedia Commons (`sources.json` gives each one's page, author, licence as the page states it and SHA-256); two candidates whose file pages credit Unsplash were dropped. In linear light, the reflection source is scaled to cover the frame, mirrored, blurred (σ from 0.08% to 1.5% of the long side), optionally doubled by a shifted, dimmer ghost, weighted by a smooth falloff across the glass, and added to the transmitted photo, which one case tints green; the sum is clipped at white as a camera clips it. Each writes the photo through glass, the truth and the reflection layer as 16-bit TIFFs, at 4,096 pixels on the long side (one at the source's full 8,160, 50 MP). They cover soft and sharp reflections, ghosts, a faint reflection that should leave the photo nearly alone, clipped lamps and skies, and tinted glass whose tint belongs to the truth.
- **Ten real photos taken through glass**: shop windows and display cases whose Commons file pages say CC0, none of them from Unsplash or Pexels, scored by blind picks.

Two cautions: Poly Haven's HDRIs and COCO are in WindowSeat's training data, so the composites use neither; and the photos' JPEG compression is part of their truth, so a method that smooths it loses a little PSNR for no visible reason. The Redlamp Bench draft for Lightroom (`research/benchmarks/bench/glass.json`) has the twelve composites, seven of the real photos and an unedited copy of one composite to check that Lightroom's TIFF export changes nothing: 20 assets, 0.93 GB. Its steps follow Adobe's [Lightroom Classic help](https://helpx.adobe.com/lightroom-classic/desktop/process-and-develop-photos/distraction-removal-reflections.html) (updated 29 September 2026): Remove › Distraction Removal, Apply in the Reflections panel, Amount at its default, Quality Best ("the full resolution"), and the setting copied to the others with Copy Settings and Paste Settings, which the help says Reflection Removal supports. The help doesn't name a version, so the step says the path hasn't been checked against 15's own menus. The help also says that "Sometimes, the +100 setting might show the reflection. If so, move the slider to -100", so the task asks whether that happened.

## 6. Recommendation

**Use WindowSeat, as an opt-in download on Macs with 32 GB or more, and apply it by subtracting its reflection layer at full resolution.**

- **The model.** WindowSeat v1.0 on Qwen-Image-Edit-2509, with the LoRA fused and the transformer quantised to 4 bits (the modulation layers at 8): 13.5 GB, the transformer in seven parts as release assets (DEC-26), labelled as using a model whose base's training data is undisclosed (DEC-23). 8 bits would add 0.7 dB on the composites for 8.5 GB more; 4 bits is the better trade for a download.
- **How it runs.** In RedlampGenerative through mlx-swift (DEC-25), beside FLUX.2 [klein] 4B: a port of the Qwen-Image transformer (60 dual-stream blocks of width 3,072, 24 heads, joint attention with 3-axis rotary positions; 20.4B parameters) and of its VAE (Wan-style causal 3D convolutions, which for one frame reduce to 2D ones, convolved in bands), one step at timestep 0.499 with the shipped prompt embedding, evaluated block by block so no command buffer runs long, and its output checked for NaN. Nothing new is needed at render time: the edit stores R_w, the reflection layer at the working size, as a 16-bit image in the package's masks folder, as a generated fill's pixels are, and the render stage subtracts it in linear light at the edit's Amount. So a photo renders the same on any Mac, with the model or without, and the Amount slider costs nothing. For a raw photo, the model would see the photo as the generative crop shows it to FLUX (scaled, rolled off and sRGB-encoded) and its output would come back through the same steps reversed; that mapping hasn't been tested with this model.
- **The full-resolution step,** with the settings measured here: the drift gain, R_w smoothed by 2 working pixels, no special handling for clipped highlights. On the GPU it is a downsample, a division, a blur, an upsample and a subtraction.
- **What to measure next:** Lightroom's results on the same set (the bench draft) and the owner's picks; near-native tiling as a Best quality, on real photos with sharp reflections (§4.3); 1,024-pixel tiles once the VAE is banded (0.4 dB better than 768 on the composites); a better drift estimate (worth up to 1 dB); and the 4-bit modulation layers (1.8 GB smaller).

**The fallback, if the owner won't ship a 13.5 GB download:** train Redlamp's own reflection LoRA on FLUX.2 [klein] 4B, which Generative Remove already downloads, with WindowSeat's recipe and its Apache-2.0 rendering pipeline fed with CC0 HDRIs and photos. PRISM shows that base gets within 0.9 dB of WindowSeat on SIR²; the LoRA would add a few hundred megabytes (an estimate) to a 2.41 GB model that runs in 6.6 GB at 1024 pixels on Macs with 16 GB. It needs a GPU for about a day (WindowSeat's figure for a 12B model on one consumer GPU) and a new tracker row. Asking PRISM's authors whether Apache-2.0 or CC BY-NC is meant costs nothing, and an Apache-2.0 answer would give the same thing without training. GFRRN (MIT) is the small model to try where neither is possible, untested here.

**What the owner decides:**

1. Whether Remove Reflections may be a 13.5 GB opt-in download for Macs with 32 GB or more (WindowSeat, DEC-23's labelling), or should wait for a FLUX.2 [klein] LoRA of Redlamp's own (a training task, a new row).
2. Whether to ask PRISM's authors about their licence.
3. The upload of the converted model as release assets, when the port exists (DEC-26).
4. Whether the result is labelled as generated, as Generative Remove's fills are: under strong reflections the model invents what the glass hid (its paper calls it inpainting), while elsewhere the photo's own pixels stay.
5. The bench task in Lightroom and the blind picks, once the parent agent has made the task.
